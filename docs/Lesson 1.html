Lesson 1: Why study calculus?

Within this lesson, we will learn what calculus is and why we study it. We will start with an example looking at how the speed of someone running is calculated, and then we will introduce the idea of a limit. We will build up your understanding by doing lots of examples, and if you have any questions then please message on the google classroom.

Section 1: Calculating speed

For this section I have made up these numbers, this is more about the understanding than the Maths. Our first question we are going to answer is “How can we calculate the speed of something?”. This might seem obvious, because if you wanted to know the speed of someone who ran a 100 meter race in 15 seconds, then you would do the following calculation: \[ Speed=\dfrac{Distance}{Time}=\dfrac{100}{15}=6.67ms^{-1}\] This means that if Bob did run a 100 m in 15 s, then his average speed would be 6.67 m/s. However, we do know that he will not have travelled the entire race at this speed. During the start of the race he will be accellerating to his top speed, and he will be at his top speed in the middle of the race once he has fully accellerated. This means during the start he will be travelling less than 6.67 m/s and at the middle of the race he will be travelling quicker than 6.67 m/s. So how would we calculate the speed of the Bob once he is exactly 15 m into the race? As we are dealing with distance, it makes sense to change the distance that we are measuring over. Instead of working out his speed for the entire race, we can work out his speed for the first 30 m, as this will give us a more accurate value of how fast he is travelling at 15 m. If it took Bob 8 s to run the first 30 meters, then we will get: \[ Speed = \dfrac{Distance}{Time}=\dfrac{30}{8}=3.75ms^{-1} \] As you can see, this 3.75 m/s is slower than 6.67 m/s which is exactly what we expected. We can also decrease the distance we measure on even more, for example we can measure the time it takes Bob to run between 10 m and 20 m. Measuring the time it takes Bob to run between 10 m and 20 m will give a more accurate value for the speed Bob is running at when he is exactly 15 m into the race. This can be seen in figure 1.

Now we can take a step back and think about what is happening. As we decrease the distance that we measure over, then the value of the speed becomes more accurate. So we want to measure over the smallest distance possible in to find the most accurate value for the speed of the person. Now we can have a go at some practice questions.

Section 2: Displacement time graphs

Note that for this section I am using displacement and velocity instead of distance and speed. For those of you who have not studied the differences between distance and displacement (or speed and velocity), then you can think of them as the same thing and you will still be able to follow along. You should now have a good understanding of how speed is calculated, now we can look at what this means on a graph. Displacement time graphs are commonly used to solve problems using speed. Displacement is displayed on the y-axis of the graph and time is displayed on the x-axis of the graph. For example, if I walked into university, then sat through a lecture and then got the bus back home, my displacement time graph would look something like the following:

From graph 1 we can see a few things. The y-axis tells us that the University is 1km away from my house, as the line becomes horizontal at the 1km mark. The x-axis is telling us that it has taken me 30 minutes (or 0.5 hours) to walk into University, then spent 1 hour at University, then it took me around 20 minutes to get home on the bus. For a displacement time graph, the gradient of the graph represents the velocity. This can be seen because the gradient of the graph when I take the bus is steeper than the gradient of the graph when I walk, which implies that I am moving quicker when I’m on the bus, and slower when I’m walking. We can also see that the gradient of the graph is zero for the duration of my lecture, which means that I am not moving while watching my lecture. The last comment I want to make about this graph is that it is not perfectly accurate. We can see that the gradients of each portion of the graph is a straight line, which implies that I am moving at a constant velocity. We know this is not true because in real life you may have to stop or speed up for certain reasons (i.e. while I’m walking I may have to stop at traffic lights or while I’m on the bus the bus might wait at a bus stop). From this graph, there are two key takeaways:

  1. The gradient of a displacement time graph represents the velocity
  2. The steeper the gradient, then the greater the velocity

Now you may have realised that with all of the calculations so far we are still using two values of the distance to measure the time over and using that to calculate speed. You may ask yourself the question “Is it possible to only using one value of the distance to calculate the speed?” and you can do this using calculus! However, before we look at that, we need to understand what a limit is.

Section 3: What is a limit?

In Maths, a limit is used to say that if we have some number x that is getting closer and closer to another number y , then x=y . For example, if we have some number x , that keeps getting closer and closer to the number 3, then we will write: \[ \lim_{x \rightarrow 3} x = 3 \] One way that I like to think of limits is by thinking of an ant on a number line. As you can see from figure 3, the ant is starting from the left and it will keep moving to the right until it reaches the number 3, then it will stop.

For this example it does not matter if the ant starts at the number -1 and move to the right (like it has in the diagram), or if the ant starts at the number 6 and moves to the left, as it will always finish at the number 3. There can be times when the value of the limit does depend on whether your increasing or decreasing with your value of x (if your ant is moving to the left or to the right). One of the practice questions I have included for this section shows this and I have included it to challenge you so do not worry if you struggle with it, as we will talk about it when we have the lesson.

All of that aside, most limits I will show you can simply be solved be a direct substitution. For example, if I have the following: \[ \lim_{x \rightarrow 2} x^2-4x+5 \] Then all I need to do to solve this is substitute in x=2 , which will give me: \[ \lim_{x \rightarrow 2} x^2-4x+5 = (2)^2-4(2)+5=4-8+5=1\]

A more visual understanding of this would be to picture the ant moving along the curve of the graph. For example, below shows the graph of \(y=x^2-4x+5\):

From this curve, we can imagine that an ant is stood to the right of two and to the left of two, with both ants making their way towards 2 but they’re walking along the curve. This can be shown in the diagram below, where the arrows represent the direction that the ants are moving:

From this curve it is easy to see what will happen to the ants. Both ants will meet at \(x=2\) where \(y=1\), just like what we saw in the previous example. Remember this technique when solving practice question 3.

We have looked at limits where we are converging to a number, but what happens when we are going off to infinity. Unfortunately, as infinity is not a number we cannot simply substitute it into an formula and solve it, so instead there are two main ways that we solve limits with infinity. Suppose I wanted to find out what \(lim_{x \rightarrow \infty} -x^3+5x^2-x-6\) is equal to. The first way would be to draw a graph:

Just by looking at the graph, we can see that it looks like as \(x\) gets bigger, then \(y\) gets smaller meaning that if \(x\) is going off to infinity, then \(y\) is going to negative infinity. We can confirm this by trying the second method. I said that we cannot substitute infinity into the formula (as infinity is not a number), however we can still just substitute in really big numbers to see what happens. We can substitite in 1000, 10000 and 100000 into \(-x^3+5x^2-x-6\) to see what it gives us:

\[ -(1000)^3+5(1000)^2-(1000)-6=-995001006\] \[ -(10000)^3+5(10000)^2-(10000)-6=-9.9950001001 \times 10^{11}\] \[ -(100000)^3+5(100000)^2-(100000)-6= -9.999500001 \times 10^{14}\]

From this, we can see that when we substitute in larger values of \(x\) then the \(y\) values decrease by larger amounts, meaning that \(\lim_{x \rightarrow \infty} -x^3+5x^2-x-6 = - \infty\).

Within our previous lesson we managed to start looking at some of the content within section 1. During the lesson I found that some of my explanations had not been as good as planned. I am writing this section just to help with some of the questions as I found that there was some confusion.

How to calculate speed

The speed of a person is defined as follows: \[ Speed = \dfrac{\text{Change in distance}}{\text{Change in time}}\] We know that the ‘Change in distance’ is going to be the length of the interval, and the ‘Change in time’ is going to be how long it took the person to go through the interval. This means we can write: \[ Speed = \dfrac{\text{Change in distance}}{\text{Change in time}} = \dfrac{\text{Distance at the end - Distance at the start}}{\text{Time at the start - Time at the end}}\] Note that when I say ‘at the start’ and ‘at the end’ I mean ‘at the start of the interval’ and ‘at the end of the interval’.

Example question

If we are trying to solve part c of the 2nd question in section 1 then we can use this formula. We know that the smallest interval around 75 m is going to give the the most accurate value for the speed. Knowing this we will consider the 70 m to 80 m interval. Now we can use the formula for speed: \[ Speed = \dfrac{\text{Distance at the end - Distance at the start}}{\text{Time at the start - Time at the end}} = \dfrac{80 - 70}{7.96 - 7.14} = \dfrac{10}{0.82} \approx 12. 20 ms^{-1}\]

Common mistakes

During the lesson some students had a go at solving part c of the 2nd question in section 1 and they made mistakes. Mistakes are brilliant because it shows that you are learning and it also helps me as I will be able to explain it better to future students. One of the mistakes made was calculating the speed for the interval 0 m to 75 m. This can be done by working out the speed for the interval 0 m to 70 m and the speed for the interval 0 m to 80 m, and then taking an average of the two. That calculation would be done like this: \[ \text{Speed for the interval 0m to 75m} = \dfrac{\text{Speed for the interval 0m and 70m}+ \text{Speed for the interval 0m and 80m}}{2} = \dfrac{\frac{70}{7.14}+\frac{80}{7.96}}{2} \approx 9.925 ms^{-1} \] The problem with this is that we are using an interval which is 75 m, so we are still including the start of the race where Bolt will be running slower.
